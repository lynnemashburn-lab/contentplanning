    The Long Way Home

    Most afternoons I take the long way home from school, and most afternoons someone asks why. The short route is a straight shot down the main road, nine minutes if the light cooperates. The long way adds a hill, two extra turns, and a stretch of sidewalk that nobody has bothered to fix. I started taking it by accident, and I kept taking it on purpose.

    The first thing the long way gave me was quiet. Nobody waits for me at the top of the hill, so there is nothing to hurry toward. The walk became the one part of the day with no bell at the end of it. After a few weeks I noticed that the problems I carried out of seventh period seemed smaller by the time I reached the broken sidewalk. Nothing about them had changed. I had simply stopped carrying them so tightly.

    The second thing it gave me was practice at paying attention. On the main road, everything is loud and the same. On the hill, small things change: a fence gets painted, a dog learns my schedule, a tree drops its leaves in a single week. Noticing those changes is a skill, and like any skill it improves when it is used. I have started to catch the same kinds of details in a paragraph or a math problem, the small thing that is different from yesterday.

    I do not think everyone needs a hill. I do think everyone needs a stretch of the day that is not built for speed. Mine happens to be eleven extra minutes on a cracked sidewalk. Yours might be a bus window, a kitchen table, or the five minutes before practice starts. I would not trade mine for the straight shot.

        Why I Stopped Checking the Grade Portal

        For most of high school I checked the grade portal the way other people check the weather. It was the first thing I opened in the morning and the last thing I looked at before bed. A quiz could move a number by a few tenths of a point, and I would feel the change before I understood it. On a bad day, one refresh could set the tone for the next six hours.

        This spring I tried a small experiment. I logged out of the portal and agreed with myself that I would look once a week, on Friday, after school. The first few days were uncomfortable. I kept reaching for my phone and finding nothing to refresh. By Wednesday I had stopped reaching.

        By the second week, something shifted. I was still doing the work, and in some classes I was doing it better, because I was thinking about the assignment instead of the number it would become. Friday's check felt like reading a report instead of watching a scoreboard.

        I still care about my grades. I just no longer let them interrupt every hour of the day. A number that updates in real time asks for real-time attention, and I have decided that mine is better spent elsewhere. The portal will still be there on Friday.
